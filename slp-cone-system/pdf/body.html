QQ002QQPart I — Functional SpecificationPart I

QQ003QQ1Purpose and scope

1.1Problem statement

A driving instructor running a practical lesson on a training pad sets each exercise by walking the pad and placing traffic cones by hand, measuring spacings by pacing or with a tape. Changing from a slalom to a parallel-parking bay means walking the pad again.

Three costs follow. In a sixty-minute lesson, ten to fifteen minutes of instruction time is spent on cone placement rather than teaching. Geometry is inconsistent between sessions and between instructors, so a trainee's performance is not comparable from one lesson to the next. And placement puts an instructor on foot in the same space in which a learner driver is operating a vehicle.

1.2What the system does

The Collaborative Training Cone System replaces manual placement with a fleet of self-driving cones. The instructor selects or draws an exercise on a tablet; the fleet arranges itself into that exercise; redrawing rearranges it. Cone geometry becomes repeatable to the centimetre, changeover takes under a minute, and the instructor never leaves the vehicle or the edge of the pad.

1.3System boundary

Inside the boundaryComprising
Cone unitsThe mobile robots, their shells, drives, sensing, power and firmware
Localisation infrastructureFixed anchors and their survey, plus the development ground-truth rig
Radio gatewayThe bridge between the coordinator host and the cone control link
CoordinatorThe software that compiles layouts, assigns cones and plans motion
Tablet applicationThe instructor's entire interface to the system

Outside the boundary: the training pad and its surface, the training vehicle, the driving syllabus, mains power and charging infrastructure, and the tablet hardware itself.

1.4Actors

ActorTypeInteraction and assumptions
InstructorPrimary human userAuthors and deploys layouts, monitors the fleet, stops it. Assumed non-technical, and assumed to be seated in or standing beside a vehicle
TechnicianSecondary human userFirst-time pad setup, calibration, charging, firmware updates, fault recovery. Assumed trained on the system
Cone unitAutonomous agentLocalises, drives, avoids its neighbours, reports its state, and protects itself when contact with the outside world goes wrong
CoordinatorSoftware agentCompiles layouts, assigns and plans, and mediates every instructor command
Trainee driverAffected party — not a userNever operates the system and may not be aware of it. Their safety constrains every requirement in §2.5

1.5Assumed operating environment

An outdoor asphalt or concrete training pad, nominally 30 × 50 m, effectively flat (gradient ≤ 5%), in daylight, dry to lightly wet, at 0–45 °C, with no overhead cover and one training vehicle present at a time.

Confirm before design freeze

These assumptions drive a large fraction of the requirements that follow — in particular the choice of localisation technology, which is the project's highest-risk subsystem. They are stated here as assumptions precisely so that they can be checked. See §10, question 2.

QQ004QQ2Feature catalogue

Fifty-three features in eight functional areas. Each carries an identifier that is used throughout the remainder of the project.

2.1F1 — Course authoring

The instructor defines exercise geometry on the tablet.

IDFeatureDescription
F1.1Freehand pathDraw a continuous path with a finger or stylus on a scaled plan of the pad
F1.2Point placementTap to place, drag to move, long-press to delete an individual cone position
F1.3Geometric primitivesInsert a parameterised straight line, arc, circle or rectangle instead of drawing by hand
F1.4Path editingInsert, move and delete control points; unlimited undo and redo
F1.5Automatic spacingDistribute cones evenly along a path at a spacing given in metres — default 2.0 m, range 0.5–20 m, settable per segment
F1.6Numeric dimensioningEnter exact dimensions instead of drawing: a 6.0 × 2.5 m parking bay, or a slalom at 9.0 m pitch
F1.7Feasibility checkValidate continuously while the layout is being drawn: cones required against cones available and healthy, every point inside the pad boundary, no two points closer than the minimum separation. Violations are reported in place
F1.8Layout transformTranslate, rotate and scale the whole layout on the pad; mirror about an axis
F1.9Reference overlayShow pad boundary, a metric grid, and optionally the previous layout in grey for comparison
Design rationale — F1.7

This is the feature that prevents the most common operational failure. Without it, an instructor discovers mid-deployment that the layout needs nine cones and the fleet has six healthy ones — at which point cones are already moving and the exercise is late. Validation belongs at authoring time, not at execution time.

2.2F2 — Layout library

IDFeatureDescription
F2.1SaveStore the current layout under a name, with author and date
F2.2LoadRecall a stored layout into the editor, or deploy it directly
F2.3Standard presetsShip a built-in, parameterised set (§3) so a new instructor is productive without drawing anything
F2.4ManageDuplicate, rename, delete; mark favourites
F2.5Import / exportExchange layouts between instructors and installations as a documented JSON file
F2.6Lesson sequenceGroup layouts into an ordered lesson; a single Next Exercise action advances the fleet from one to the next
Design rationale — F2.6

This feature expresses the value of the whole project better than any other. A single button that reconfigures an entire training pad between exercises makes the case to an assessment panel far more effectively than any one layout can. It is inexpensive to build once F2.2 and F3.2 exist.

2.3F3 — Deployment and execution

IDFeatureDescription
F3.1PreviewBefore any cone moves, show which cone is assigned to which target and the path each will take, with an estimated time to completion
F3.2ExecuteCommit the layout; the fleet moves
F3.3Live progressPer-cone state and remaining distance, an overall percentage, and an updating ETA
F3.4Pause / resumeFreeze the fleet in place and continue from the same assignment
F3.5AbortStop the operation, offering stop-in-place or return-to-previous-layout
F3.6READY signalAn unambiguous fleet-wide indication — on the tablet and on the cones — that every cone is within tolerance and has settled, so the instructor knows the exercise may begin
F3.7GatherSend every cone to a defined collection area for storage or charging
F3.8Re-deploy displacedDetect a cone knocked out of position and, on instructor confirmation, drive it back to its target without disturbing the rest of the layout
Safety rationale — F3.6

READY is a safety feature, not a convenience. The instructor must never have to guess whether the fleet has finished moving before releasing a vehicle onto the pad. It is specified as a full-width banner rather than a small icon for exactly this reason (§5).

Design rationale — F3.8

Cones in driver training get hit; that is what they are for. A system that requires a full redeployment after every contact is worse than manual placement, because the instructor must now interrupt the lesson and wait for six cones. A system that quietly nudges one cone back into place is better than anything a human can do without stopping the vehicle.

2.4F4 — Monitoring

IDFeatureDescription
F4.1Live mapReal-time cone positions on the pad plan, with target positions shown as ghosts and deviation drawn as a vector
F4.2Per-cone statusIdentifier, state, battery percentage, link quality, position error, and time since last contact

2.5F5 — Safety

Every requirement in this group is mandatory. None may be traded away for schedule, and none may be deferred to a later revision.

IDFeatureDescription
F5.1Emergency stopA single action stops the entire fleet. Available as a permanent on-screen control and as a physical handheld button independent of the tablet
F5.2Comms watchdogA cone that receives no valid command for 500 ms stops itself. Safety does not depend on the radio working
F5.3GeofenceA cone refuses to drive outside the configured pad boundary regardless of the commanded target
F5.4Tip-over detectionA cone that is knocked over cuts motor drive within 1 s and reports it
F5.5Obstacle reflexA cone that detects an object within 40 cm ahead brakes, independently of the central planner
F5.6Vehicle-present interlockWhile the instructor has flagged a vehicle as active on the pad, the fleet will not move. Overriding requires a deliberate, separate confirmation
F5.7Speed limitingConfigurable maximum speed, enforced in firmware, defaulting to 0.8 m/s
F5.8Movement conspicuityA moving cone shows a flashing light and sounds an intermittent tone
Addition to the proposal — F5.6

The vehicle-present interlock does not appear in the approved proposal and should be added to it. The system moves robots across a surface on which a learner driver is operating a car. An explicit interlock, owned by the instructor rather than inferred by the system, is the single most important safety feature in this specification — and it is the first thing an assessment panel will ask about.

Note the deliberate division of responsibility: F5.1, F5.6 and F5.7 are policy, set by a human. F5.2, F5.3, F5.4 and F5.5 are reflexes, implemented on the cone. The reflexes must remain effective when the coordinator, the radio or the tablet has failed. No safety property in this specification depends on a working radio link.

2.6F6 — Cone autonomy

Functions each cone performs on its own.

IDFeatureDescription
F6.1Self-localisationContinuously estimate its own position and heading on the pad

Design rationale — F6.8

Identify costs almost nothing and will be used constantly. The moment there are six identical cones on a pad and cone 4 reports a fault, somebody has to work out which one cone 4 is. Without this feature that means reading serial numbers off the underside of six units.

Consequence — fleet size

The approved proposal does not state how many cones the fleet contains, yet that number determines cost, the pad area required, and the difficulty of the coordination problem. The presets above require between four and twelve.

A fleet of six is adopted as the prototype baseline. It covers the majority of the presets outright and degrades gracefully on the rest by increasing spacing. Two constraints follow, and both are binding on Part II: the software must impose no fixed fleet limit and must be verified in simulation at twelve (NFR-14); and where a layout needs more cones than are available, F1.7 must say so at authoring time and offer to increase the spacing instead of failing.

QQ006QQ4Use cases

UC-1 — Set up an exercise before a lesson

Actor: Instructor  ·  Precondition: Fleet powered, localised and in the gather area  ·  Frequency: Several times per day
  1. The instructor opens the tablet application; the live map shows six cones ready.
  2. The instructor opens the layout library and selects Slalom. F2.3
  3. The instructor adjusts the pitch from 9.0 m to 12.0 m; the preview updates live. F1.5, F1.6
  4. The system validates: five cones needed, six available, all points inside the boundary. F1.7
  5. The instructor taps Preview; the system shows the assignment and an estimated 38 s. F3.1
  6. The instructor confirms no vehicle is on the pad and taps Execute. F3.2, F5.6
  7. Cones move; the map shows live progress. F3.3
  8. All cones settle within tolerance; READY is displayed and the cones stop flashing. F3.6
  9. The instructor begins the lesson.
Alternate flows 4a. Fewer healthy cones than the layout needs → the system offers to increase the spacing or deploy a reduced version, and will not offer Execute until this is resolved.
6a. The vehicle interlock is set → Execute is disabled, with the reason shown.
7a. A cone stalls → an alert is raised; the remaining cones complete; the instructor may retry that cone, exclude it, or abort.

UC-2 — Change exercise mid-lesson

Actor: Instructor  ·  Precondition: A layout is deployed and a lesson is in progress  ·  This is the project's headline demonstration
  1. The instructor stops the vehicle clear of the pad and sets the vehicle interlock to clear. F5.6
  2. The instructor taps Next Exercise in the active lesson sequence. F2.6
  3. The system computes the assignment from current positions to the new layout; cones already near a target keep it.
  4. The instructor taps Execute; the fleet reconfigures in under 60 s. NFR-3
  5. READY appears and the lesson continues.
Why this one matters Every other use case demonstrates a capability. This one demonstrates the benefit — the ten to fifteen minutes of lesson time recovered in §1.1. It should be the sequence performed live at the defence.

UC-3 — A cone is knocked over during an exercise

Actors: Cone, then Instructor  ·  Trigger: Vehicle contact  ·  Frequency: Routine, not exceptional
  1. A vehicle contacts a cone; the cone tips.
  2. The cone detects the tilt within 1 s, cuts motor drive and reports TIPPED. F5.4
  3. The tablet raises an alert identifying which cone, and shows it on the map. F4.4
  4. The instructor pauses the lesson and rights the cone by hand.
  5. The cone re-localises and reports IDLE.
  6. The instructor taps Re-deploy; only that cone returns to its target. F3.8
  7. READY returns.
Design consequence This use case imposes a hard constraint on Part II: righting a cone by hand must be sufficient to recover it. No tablet interaction, no power cycle, no re-pairing, no technician. Any design in which recovery needs more than step 4 fails this use case.

UC-4 — A cone's battery runs out mid-session

Actors: Cone, Coordinator, Instructor  ·  Precondition: A session is in progress
  1. The cone reaches 20% charge → a warning alert is raised; the cone continues. F6.7
  2. The cone reaches 10% → it refuses further assignments, informs the coordinator and requests recall.
  3. The coordinator excludes it from future assignments and warns the instructor that the effective fleet is now five.
  4. The instructor sends that cone to gather; F1.7 now validates all subsequent layouts against five cones.
Design consequence Fleet size is a runtime quantity, not a configuration constant. Every feature that depends on it — F1.7 above all — must read the count of currently healthy cones rather than the count of registered ones.

UC-5 — End of day

Actors: Instructor, Technician
  1. The instructor taps Gather. F3.7
  2. All cones drive to the collection area, arrange themselves in a row and stop.
  3. The technician switches them off and puts them on charge.

UC-6 — First-time installation on a new pad

Actor: Technician  ·  Frequency: Once per site, plus after any anchor is moved
  1. Mount four anchors on poles at the pad corners and survey their positions.
  2. Enter the anchor coordinates, define the origin, trace the boundary and mark the gather area. F7.1
  3. Register each cone unit. F7.2
  4. Run the guided calibration routines. F7.3
  5. Run the verification layout and confirm the achieved accuracy meets §6. F8.2

5.1Interaction constraints

These follow from the operating environment in §1.5, not from taste. Each one is testable and each one is a defect if violated.

  • Usable in direct sunlight. High contrast throughout; large type; no thin light-grey text on white.
  • Usable with gloves and wet hands. Touch targets no smaller than 48 px; no hover states; no gestures requiring precision or two hands.
  • Usable one-handed while standing beside a vehicle.
  • No destructive action without confirmation — with exactly one exception. E-STOP must be instant and must never ask.
  • Fleet state readable in under two seconds from across a vehicle cabin. This is the reason READY is a banner and not an icon, and the reason the fleet summary is a row of counts rather than a list to be read.

Emphasis — NFR-11

The proposal asks for "a stable and durable mechanical structure that protects the internal components while maintaining the appearance and function of a training cone." In engineering terms that means three things: the shell is sacrificial and compliant, the electronics are mechanically isolated from shell deformation, and the centre of mass is low enough that the unit either stays upright or is trivially righted by hand — a requirement that UC-3 makes binding. The severity of this requirement is the single largest driver of the mechanical design, and it is open question 5 in §10.

7.2Minimum demonstrable product

The subset that must work live, on hardware, at the defence:

The four-minute sequence

Four cones. The instructor loads the slalom preset, adjusts the spacing, previews and executes. The cones cross the area simultaneously without colliding and settle within tolerance. READY appears. The instructor loads a parking bay and executes again; the cones reconfigure. A cone is deliberately knocked over; the system detects it, alerts, and re-deploys it once it has been righted by hand. The instructor presses E-STOP mid-motion and everything halts.

That single sequence exercises F1.5, F1.7, F2.3, F3.1, F3.2, F3.6, F3.8, F4.1, F4.4, F5.1, F5.4 and F6.1 – F6.4 — effectively the whole Must set — in about four minutes, and it tells the story of §1.1 without a slide. Build toward this sequence specifically, and rehearse it. Anything that does not serve it, or serve the report, is not on the critical path.

QQ012QQ10Open questions for the advisor

These five questions change the specification. They should be closed before the design freeze in week 2, because each of them invalidates work if answered late.

#QuestionWhat changes with the answer
1What is the prototype fleet size? §3 argues for six.Budget, pad area, and the difficulty of the coordination problem. If the budget covers fewer, the balance is simulated and §7.2 still holds — it needs only four
2What are the pad dimensions and surface, and is there a specific site?The choice of localisation technology, which is the project's highest-risk subsystem (§11.2)
3Is an indoor demonstration acceptable?The largest single lever in the project. Indoors, an overhead camera gives roughly 1 cm accuracy for the cost of one camera, and the highest-risk subsystem becomes cheap, accurate and quick. Outdoors, that fails and UWB is required
4Is heading control required, or is position sufficient?Cones are rotationally symmetric, so heading matters only for asymmetric markers. If it is not required, NFR-2 and a substantial slice of the localisation work disappear
5Must a cone survive being driven over, or only bumped?The severity of NFR-11, and with it the entire mechanical concept — shell material, isolation strategy and mass budget
Recommended action

Questions 3 and 4 together can remove the majority of the project's technical risk at no cost to its academic value. They should be put to the advisor first, and in that order.

QQ014QQ11The four decisions that define the architecture

Everything else follows from these four. Each is argued rather than asserted, because a defence panel will ask why not the other one.

11.1Where does the intelligence live?

OptionAssessment
Fully centralised
coordinator computes wheel speeds; cones are radio-controlled motors		Simple to reason about, but every packet loss becomes a control-loop dropout, and F5.2 cannot be satisfied on the cone because the cone has no notion of its own goal
Fully decentralised
each cone runs local swarm rules — potential fields, flocking		Superficially the more impressive "swarm" answer, but position assignment becomes emergent and therefore unrepeatable. A driving exercise is a specified geometry, not an emergent one, and NFR-1 is stated in centimetres
Hybrid
coordinator does assignment and global avoidance; cone does pose control and reflexes		Chosen. See below

The coordinator owns everything global — who goes where, whose path yields to whose. Each cone owns everything local and fast: wheel PID, goal seeking, emergency stop, tip detection, communications watchdog.

Why this split, specifically

Safety does not cross the radio. If the coordinator dies, cones coast to a stop on their own watchdog (F5.2). Every reflex in §2.5 remains effective with the link down, which is exactly the property NFR-9 demands.

It maps onto the team. One student can own the coordinator without touching firmware, and another can own firmware without touching the planner — see §15. An architecture that forces two students into the same file every week is a scheduling hazard, not just an engineering one.

Three consequences the team must plan around

ConsequenceDetail and mitigation
UWB gives position, not headingRecover heading from the gyroscope, corrected by the direction of travel — a differential-drive robot moving forward is, by construction, pointing where it is going. Correct gyro bias whenever speed exceeds 0.3 m/s. Contingency: a second tag 25 cm from the first gives heading directly; it doubles tag cost, so it is the fallback, not the baseline. Open question 4 in §10 may remove this problem entirely
Update rate falls as cones are addedWith two-way ranging, one fix costs roughly 3 ms per anchor, so about 12 ms for four anchors. Under TDMA that is ≈ 13 Hz at six cones and ≈ 8 Hz at ten. Acceptable for the target fleet. Scaling path: migrate to TDoA — tags transmit, synchronised anchors listen — which scales to 100+ tags. Document this in the report; forward-looking analysis of a limit you have measured is worth more than avoiding the limit
Cones are lowA cone stands about 50 cm tall, so its tag sits near the ground where multipath off asphalt is worst. Mount anchors on 3–4 m poles, and never place all four near-collinear — poor geometry (high GDOP) ruins trilateration even with perfect range measurements
Use the camera anyway — as an instrument, not as the product

During development, an indoor rig with a 1080p camera at 6 m and an ArUco marker on each cone lid yields roughly 1 cm absolute pose. That is the reference against which the UWB + EKF stack is measured to produce the numbers NFR-1 requires. A 90° camera at 6 m covers about 12 × 9 m; at 1920 px across 12 m, one pixel is 6.25 mm, and sub-pixel marker fitting does the rest.

Building a validation instrument, rather than eyeballing a tape measure, is what separates a strong capstone from an average one — and it is the same rig that answers open question 3 if the project moves indoors.

11.4How do cones avoid each other?

Six cones crossing a 20 m pad simultaneously is a genuine multi-robot motion planning problem, not an implementation detail. NFR-7 permits zero collisions.

ApproachAssessment
Artificial potential fieldsQuick to write, but suffers local minima and oscillates in narrow passes. Not defensible against a panel that knows the literature
Prioritised / sequenced planning
one cone moves at a time		Trivially safe, and trivially fails NFR-3: six cones crossing 20 m one after another exhausts the 60 s budget on travel alone
Time–space reservation
grid plus time windows		Sound, but the discretisation is coarse relative to a 15 cm accuracy target and replanning after a disturbance is expensive
ORCA — reciprocal velocity obstaclesChosen. Continuous rather than discretised, runs at kilohertz rates for six agents, collision-free under its stated assumptions, and is the standard citation in the field

ORCA assumes holonomic agents. Differential-drive cones are not holonomic, so the ORCA output velocity is wrapped by a unicycle controller that converts a desired (vx, vy) into (v, ω) with a bounded turn rate — the standard treatment, in which the effective agent radius is inflated to cover the turning transient.

At 0.8 m/s, 190 ms is 15 cm of travel the planner cannot see. The safety radius must cover it:

Derived parameters

rsafety = rcone (0.20 m) + v · tstale (0.15 m) + margin (0.20 m) ≈ 0.55 m → use 0.60 m
vmax = 0.80 m/s during coordinated motion, reduced to 0.30 m/s within 1 m of the goal (which also serves NFR-1, since terminal accuracy degrades with approach speed)

Independently of all of this, each cone runs a reflex layer: if the forward time-of-flight sensor reads under 40 cm, brake — regardless of what the coordinator says (F5.5). ORCA is the plan; the reflex is the seatbelt.

Why bottleneck assignment rather than the usual Hungarian minimum-sum

The layout is not ready until the last cone arrives (F3.6). Minimising the total distance travelled can leave one cone with a long haul while five sit idle; minimising the maximum single distance is what actually minimises reconfiguration time, which is what NFR-3 measures. Solve it by binary search over a distance threshold with a bipartite feasibility check at each step.

Implement minimum-sum first — in Python it is one call to scipy.optimize.linear_sum_assignment — then measure it, then show the bottleneck variant beating it on NFR-3. That comparison is a report figure on its own, and it costs an afternoon.

12.2Cone firmware — state machine

12.3Control cascade on the cone

Three nested loops, each at its own rate.

LoopRateInputOutput
Wheel velocity PID (× 2)200 HzEncoder counts, via the ESP32 PCNT hardware countersPWM duty
Unicycle pose controller50 HzEKF pose and target posev, ω
Command and avoidance10 HzESP-NOW setpointTarget pose or velocity
Two implementation notes that will otherwise cost a week each

Use the PCNT peripheral for quadrature decoding, not pin interrupts. At 1.0 m/s with a 12 CPR encoder behind a 30:1 gearbox, each wheel generates roughly 4 kHz of edges. Interrupt-driven counting will steal cycles from the 200 Hz control loop and the symptom will look like a tuning problem rather than a resourcing one.

Asphalt needs friction compensation. Add a static feed-forward term to the PID output so the wheel begins turning at low commands instead of stalling inside the deadband. Without it, terminal approach at 0.3 m/s (§11.4) will stick short of the target and NFR-1 will fail for reasons that have nothing to do with localisation.

ROS 2 is the more academically impressive answer and it supplies Nav2, tf2, rviz and Gazebo at no cost. It is not recommended here, for two reasons. This decision flips entirely on one fact: if any student already knows ROS 2 well, revisit it immediately. The argument above is about the cost of learning it, not about its merits.

QQ017QQ14Development strategy

14.1Build the simulator first

This is the most important process recommendation in this document.

Write the coordinator against a cone interface, then provide two implementations of it: the serial gateway, and a simulator that integrates differential-drive kinematics with injected noise — UWB jitter, wheel slip, packet loss, latency. The planner cannot tell them apart.

What it buysWhy it matters here
Three of four roles never wait on the machine shopThe single most common way a four-person hardware capstone loses a month
Algorithms are debugged at twelve cones on a laptopThen demonstrated on six real ones — and NFR-14 is satisfied as a by-product
Failures become repeatable tests30% packet loss, a cone that stops answering, an anchor going down: all reproducible on demand, none of them reproducible on hardware
A demonstrable deliverable in its own rightIf hardware slips, the simulator still shows the coordination working — the project degrades instead of failing

14.2Build the measurement instrument early

The overhead-camera ground-truth rig (§11.2) and the F8.2 accuracy log are prerequisites for the evidence in §8, not by-products of it. Both should exist by week 8 of the first semester. From that point, every test run generates report material automatically and at no marginal cost.

14.3Integrate in this order, and not out of it

One cone → two cones → six cones. Two cones is where collision avoidance first becomes real, and it is a far larger step than two to six. Order parts for six, but build and prove two.
